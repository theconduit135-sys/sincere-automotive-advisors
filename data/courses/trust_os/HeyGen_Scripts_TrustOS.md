## MODULE 1: The Foundation — Understanding the Trust
~360 words | ~2:15 runtime

Everything in this course starts with the trust. Not the LLC. Not the bank accounts. The trust. Because the trust is the foundation that everything else sits on — and if you don't understand what it is and what it does, nothing else will make sense.

A trust is a legal agreement. It has three parties. The Grantor — the person who creates it and funds it. The Trustee — the person who manages the assets held inside it. And the Beneficiary — the person or people who benefit from those assets. In most personal trust setups, you are all three. You create it, you manage it, and you benefit from it during your lifetime.

The type of trust we are building is a revocable living trust. Revocable means you can change it, amend it, or dissolve it at any time. Living means you create it while you're alive, not through a will after you die. This is the most flexible and practical trust structure for most people.

Here is what a revocable living trust actually does. It holds title to your assets. Instead of your home being titled in your name, it's titled in the name of the trust. Same for your LLC interests, your investment accounts, your vehicles — whatever you choose to place inside it. You still control all of it. You still use it. But legally, the trust is the owner.

Why does that matter? Two reasons. First, when you die, assets in the trust do not go through probate. Probate is the court-supervised process that determines who gets your property. It is public, slow, and expensive. Trust assets bypass it entirely. Second, it creates a separation between you personally and the assets you're protecting — which becomes even more powerful when you pair it with an LLC.

In this module, you will learn what goes into a trust agreement, what each section means, and how to draft your own. We keep it plain and practical. You do not need an attorney to understand this — you need the right information.

This is that information. Let's get into it.

## MODULE 3: Get the Numbers — EINs from the IRS
~330 words | ~2:05 runtime

Every entity in your structure needs its own identification number. Your trust needs one. Your LLC needs one. And getting these numbers correctly is one of the steps most people mess up — because they either skip it, do it in the wrong order, or apply for the wrong type.

An Employer Identification Number — EIN — is issued by the IRS. It is a nine-digit number that functions like a Social Security Number for a business or trust entity. You use it to open bank accounts, file taxes, and establish the entity as a separate financial identity from you personally.

Here is why this step matters beyond paperwork. When your trust and LLC each have their own EIN, they exist as distinct entities in the eyes of the IRS and in the eyes of every financial institution you approach. That distinction is what makes it possible to open separate accounts, maintain clean records, and demonstrate that your structure is real — not just a paper arrangement designed to obscure your personal finances.

In this module you will learn how to apply for an EIN for your revocable living trust. You will learn how to apply for a separate EIN for your LLC. We walk through the IRS online application process step by step, including how to answer the questions correctly so your application is approved without delays or rejections.

We also cover what to do if your trust EIN application gets flagged, how to handle the IRS call-in process if online application isn't available to you, and how to store your EIN documentation properly.

This module is direct and practical. The IRS process has specific language and specific questions — and knowing how to answer them correctly saves you hours of confusion and weeks of waiting.

Your EINs are the keys to everything that comes next.

## MODULE 5: Fund the Trust — Move Your Assets In
~365 words | ~2:20 runtime

Having a trust is not the same as having a funded trust. This is a distinction most people miss — and it is the difference between a structure that works and a document that does nothing.

A trust only protects what is inside it. If your home is still titled in your personal name, the trust does not protect it. If your LLC interest is still listed under your Social Security Number, the trust does not hold it. The act of funding — moving assets into the trust — is what activates the protection.

This module is about how to do that correctly.

We start with real estate, because it is usually the highest-value asset people are trying to protect. Funding your trust with real property means transferring the deed from your personal name into the name of the trust. That process varies slightly by state, but the core mechanics are the same: a new deed is prepared and recorded with the county. We walk through that process step by step.

We then cover business interests. If you own an LLC, the membership interest — your ownership stake — needs to be assigned to the trust. That is done through an Assignment of Membership Interest document and updated in the LLC's records. We cover how to draft it and where it gets filed.

We also cover financial accounts — investment accounts, savings, and any other assets you want the trust to hold. Most financial institutions have a process for retitling accounts into a trust. We walk through what that looks like and what documentation they typically require.

Finally, we address what not to put in the trust. Some assets — like retirement accounts and certain insurance policies — have specific rules around beneficiary designations that make trust ownership counterproductive. We cover those exceptions clearly so you don't create a tax problem while trying to build a protection strategy.

By the end of this module, your trust will hold real assets. Not hypothetically. Actually.

## MODULE 7: Tax Positioning — How the Structure Is Taxed
~350 words | ~2:15 runtime

One of the most common questions people have about this structure is: how does it affect my taxes? It is a fair question, and the answer matters — because the tax treatment of a trust and LLC combination is one of the things that makes it powerful when it is set up correctly, and one of the things that can create problems when it is not.

Let's start with the trust. A revocable living trust is what the IRS calls a grantor trust. That means for federal tax purposes, the trust is not a separate taxpayer. All income generated by trust assets is reported on your personal return — your 1040 — under your Social Security Number. The trust does not file its own return while you are alive and serving as Trustee. This is straightforward, and it is one of the reasons a revocable trust is easy to operate from a tax standpoint.

The LLC is where tax positioning gets more interesting. By default, a single-member LLC is a disregarded entity — its income and expenses flow directly to your personal return. A multi-member LLC defaults to partnership taxation. But you can make elections that change how the LLC is taxed. The most common is electing S Corporation status, which allows you to separate your business income between salary and distributions — which can produce significant self-employment tax savings at the right income level.

In this module we walk through each of these tax scenarios, what they mean for your return, and how to make the right election for your situation. We also cover deductible business expenses, the home office deduction if applicable, depreciation on business assets, and how to work effectively with your CPA when they see this structure for the first time.

This course does not provide tax advice — your CPA does that. But this module will make sure you walk into that conversation informed and prepared.

Understanding your tax position is not optional. It is part of operating the structure correctly.
