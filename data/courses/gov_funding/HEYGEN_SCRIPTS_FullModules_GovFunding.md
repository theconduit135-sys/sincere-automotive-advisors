## MODULE 1 — Define Your Business and Money Target

Module 1 has two jobs. By the time you finish, you will have a one-paragraph business description and a precise funding goal statement. Both of them need to exist on paper before you apply for anything.

The first job is your business description. Every grant agency, lender, and incentive program will ask you the same question in different ways: what does your business do? You need one answer that is clear, specific, and professional. Not a pitch. Not inspirational language. A description.

A strong business description answers five questions in a single paragraph: What do you sell or provide? Who are your customers? Where do you operate? What problem do you solve? And what makes the business viable? Here is what strong looks like: "Harvest Table is a catering company based in Memphis, Tennessee, that provides corporate lunch service and event catering to mid-size businesses and nonprofit organizations. We specialize in locally sourced meals that meet dietary accommodation standards, serving clients who need reliable, high-quality catering without an in-house kitchen team. The business is launching with two anchor clients and a clear referral pipeline." Four sentences. Professional. Specific. That is the standard.

Write your rough draft now. Use the AI prompt in Lesson 1.1 to sharpen it. Save the final version — you will paste it into every application and lender form from this point forward.

The second job is your funding goal statement. Vague goals get denied. Specific ones get funded. You need to answer three questions with precision: how much do you need, what exactly will you use it for, and what result will it produce?

Start with a line-item cost list — every expense you need to fund in your first ninety days with a realistic dollar amount next to each one. Total it. That is your funding target. Then write one paragraph: the total amount, a breakdown of how it will be used, and the measurable outcome — clients served, revenue projected, jobs created.

Here is the difference. Weak: "I need fifty thousand dollars to grow my business." Strong: "I am seeking fifty thousand dollars to cover equipment at twenty-eight thousand, three months of operating expenses at fifteen thousand, and initial marketing at seven thousand — enabling me to serve twelve clients within ninety days and generate sixty-five hundred dollars per month by month four." One of those gets a lender's attention. The other gets filed away.

Use the AI prompt in Lesson 1.2 to build and polish your funding goal statement. Save it. These two documents are the foundation of everything that follows in this course. Build them once, build them right, and move to Module 2.

## MODULE 2 — Start, Buy, or Expand? Pick the Right Play

Module 2 has one objective: you pick a path and you commit to it. Starting a business, buying an existing one, or expanding what you already have. That choice determines which programs you qualify for, what risks you are taking on, and how fast you can realistically move. You need to commit before anything else makes sense.

Starting a new business gives you full control — over structure, brand, and direction. Grant programs at the federal and state level actively target new business formation, especially in underserved communities, rural areas, and targeted industries. The challenge is no revenue history, which limits your access to conventional financing early on. Grants are your strongest play in the beginning. Bank loans become more accessible after twelve to twenty-four months of documented business activity.

Buying an existing business gives you immediate cash flow, an established customer base, and documented financials — exactly what lenders need to say yes. The SBA 7(a) loan program was built for acquisitions and can finance up to ninety percent of the purchase price when the target business has strong financials.

Expanding a business you already own typically gets you to capital fastest. You have tax returns, financials, and operating history. State incentive programs heavily favor businesses that are creating jobs or making capital investments in equipment and facilities.

Then commit. One path for the next thirty to sixty days. Chasing all three at once guarantees progress on none. Pick your play and run it.
