## MODULE 0 — Make Your Business Real
~330 words | Estimated runtime: 2:05–2:15

Before any grant application, before any government account, before any funding conversation — your business has to exist as a legal entity. That is what this module is about.

Most people who want to access government funding make the same mistake: they try to apply before their business is properly set up. And they either get rejected outright or they get flagged during verification because their business registration, their EIN, and their bank account don't match. Government programs are not forgiving of that kind of inconsistency.

We are going to make sure that does not happen to you.

In this module you will form your LLC — or verify that your existing business entity is set up correctly. We walk through how to file your Articles of Organization with your state, what your business name needs to look like, and what the registration process costs and requires in most states.

Once your LLC is formed, we cover your Employer Identification Number. Your EIN is issued by the IRS and is required for everything that follows — business banking, SAM.gov registration, grant applications, and SBA programs. We walk through the IRS online application step by step so you come out of this module with your number in hand.

We also cover your NAICS code — the classification code that identifies what kind of business you operate. This code matters more than most people realize. It determines which federal programs you qualify for, which contracts you can bid on, and how you are categorized in every government database. Choosing it correctly is not optional.

By the end of Module 0, you will have a legally formed business with an EIN and a correctly assigned NAICS code. That is the foundation everything else is built on.

Do not skip this module. Do not rush through it. Get it right.

Let's get your business real.

## MODULE 1 — Business Bank Account
~325 words | Estimated runtime: 2:00–2:10

Your business bank account is not just a place to hold money. It is proof that your business is real.

Government programs, grant reviewers, and SBA loan officers all want to see that your business has a legitimate financial infrastructure. A business checking account in your LLC's name — not your personal name, not a personal account you use for business — signals that you are operating a real entity, not just calling yourself a business.

In this module you will open a dedicated business bank account under your LLC's name and EIN. We walk through which types of banks and credit unions are most business-friendly, what documents you need to bring, and what to say when the banker asks questions about your business.

We also cover the distinction between a business checking account and a business savings account, when to have both, and how to structure your accounts so incoming grant funds, operating revenue, and reserved funds don't get mixed together. That separation matters when you are reporting to grant administrators or applying for additional funding.

This module also addresses online banks and fintech business accounts — options like Mercury, Relay, and Bluevine that are specifically built for small businesses and have streamlined account opening processes. These can be useful if your local banking options are limited or slow.

One thing to keep in mind: some government disbursements and SBA programs have specific requirements around where funds are received. We cover what to watch for so your account setup does not create a problem later.

Your business bank account is one of the first things any funder will ask about. Have it ready before you start applying for anything.

By the end of this module, your business will have a real operating account under the right name and the right tax ID.

That is the infrastructure grants and funding programs expect to see.

## MODULE 2 — Business Credit Profile
~340 words | Estimated runtime: 2:05–2:15

Most people know their personal credit score. Very few know their business credit profile — and that gap costs them access to funding they would otherwise qualify for.

Business credit is a separate system from personal credit. It is maintained by business credit bureaus — primarily Dun & Bradstreet, Equifax Business, and Experian Business — and it scores your business entity based on its payment history, credit utilization, and time in file. A strong business credit profile can unlock funding opportunities that do not touch your personal score at all.

In this module you will learn how business credit is built and how to start building yours from day one.

The first step is getting your business listed in the right databases. That starts with obtaining a DUNS number from Dun & Bradstreet — a unique identifier that the federal government uses to track businesses and that many grant programs require. We walk through how to get yours for free through the SAM.gov registration process.

We then cover how to establish your first business trade lines — vendor accounts and net-30 accounts that report to business credit bureaus. These are accounts that extend you a small line of credit and report your payment history, building your business credit file over time. We identify starter vendors that are accessible to new businesses with no prior business credit history.

We also cover what not to do. Using your personal credit for business purchases, failing to pay vendors on time, or applying for too many credit products at once can all damage a business credit profile before it is fully established.

Business credit is not built overnight. But if you start now and follow the steps in this module, you will have a growing business credit profile within sixty to ninety days — one that makes every funding application stronger.

Build it from the beginning. By the time you need it, it will be there.

## MODULE 3 — SAM.gov Registration
~335 words | Estimated runtime: 2:05–2:15

If you want to access federal grants or federal contracts, you need to be registered in SAM.gov. There is no workaround. It is a requirement.

SAM stands for System for Award Management. It is the federal government's central database for businesses that want to do business with the government — whether that means receiving grant money, bidding on federal contracts, or qualifying for specific federal programs. If you are not in SAM, you do not exist in the eyes of the federal procurement and grant-making system.

In this module we walk through the SAM.gov registration process step by step. It is not complicated, but it has specific fields and sequences that can cause confusion if you do not know what to expect.

You will need your LLC's legal name — exactly as it appears on your state registration. You will need your EIN. You will need your NAICS code. And you will need a DUNS number from Dun & Bradstreet, which we covered in the previous module. SAM.gov is in the process of transitioning to a UEI — Unique Entity Identifier — system, and we cover what that means for new registrants.

We also cover the annual renewal requirement. SAM.gov registrations expire after twelve months and must be renewed. We show you how to set a reminder so your registration stays active and does not lapse at a critical moment.

One important note: the SAM.gov registration process is free. There are third-party services that charge fees to complete this registration on your behalf. You do not need them. This module gives you everything you need to complete it yourself.

SAM.gov registration typically takes seven to ten business days to activate. We start here early in the course specifically so it is fully active by the time you are ready to apply for contracts and grants.

This is a required step. Get it done.

## MODULE 4 — Government Grants
~350 words | Estimated runtime: 2:10–2:20

Now that your business is registered, your accounts are open, and your SAM.gov profile is active — it is time to talk about grants.

A grant is money that does not have to be paid back. It is not a loan. It is not an investment. It is funding provided by a government agency, a foundation, or a program designed to support businesses in specific categories — and if your business qualifies, it is yours to apply for.

In this module we cover where government grants actually come from and how to find the ones your business is eligible for.

Grants.gov is the primary federal portal for grant opportunities. It lists thousands of active grant programs across every federal agency. We walk through how to search Grants.gov effectively — how to filter by NAICS code, by eligibility type, by program category — so you are not scrolling through irrelevant results.

We also cover state-level grant programs, which vary significantly by state but often have less competition than federal programs and faster disbursement timelines. We show you how to find your state's small business development resources and identify active programs.

In addition to Grants.gov, we cover sector-specific grant sources: programs specifically for minority-owned businesses, women-owned businesses, veteran-owned businesses, rural businesses, and businesses in specific industries like technology, agriculture, and manufacturing. If your business qualifies for any of these designations, we show you how to get certified and how to use that certification to access dedicated funding streams.

We also cover the reality of grant timelines. Most grants are competitive and take time — from application to award can be sixty days, ninety days, or longer depending on the program. Grant funding is not fast money. It is strategic money. We show you how to build a pipeline of applications so you always have submissions in process.

By the end of this module you will know exactly where to look and what you are looking for.

## MODULE 5 — Grant Application Writing
~345 words | Estimated runtime: 2:10–2:20

Knowing where to find grants is one thing. Writing an application that wins is something else.

Most small business grant applications fail not because the business is unqualified, but because the application is weak. The narrative is vague. The budget is incomplete. The business plan does not clearly connect to the grant's stated priorities. Grant reviewers read hundreds of applications — and the ones that get funded are the ones that make the reviewer's job easy.

This module teaches you how to write a competitive grant application.

We start with the project narrative — the written section that explains who you are, what your business does, how you will use the funds, and why your business is the right fit for this grant. We walk through the structure of a strong narrative, how to align your language with the grant's stated goals, and what to avoid — including common mistakes that signal an inexperienced applicant.

We then cover the budget section. Every grant application requires a budget that accounts for every dollar being requested. We show you how to build a grant budget that is detailed, defensible, and consistent with your narrative.

We also cover supporting documents — most applications require business registration documents, bank statements, financial projections, tax returns, or letters of support. We walk through what to prepare in advance so you are not scrambling when a deadline approaches.

This module also introduces AI-assisted grant writing. You will learn how to use AI tools to research grant priorities, draft narrative language, and review your applications before submission. AI does not write a winning application for you — but used correctly, it dramatically accelerates the drafting process and helps you identify gaps before they cost you the award.

Grant writing is a skill. The more applications you submit, the better you get. But starting with the right framework puts you ahead of most of the competition on your very first submission.

## MODULE 6 — SBA Programs
~340 words | Estimated runtime: 2:05–2:15

The Small Business Administration is one of the most significant sources of business funding in the United States — and most small business owners interact with it only when they think about loans. That is too narrow a view.

The SBA offers loans, yes. But it also offers surety bond programs, investment programs through Small Business Investment Companies, technical assistance through its network of Small Business Development Centers, and access to government contracts through its certification programs. This module covers the full SBA ecosystem so you can identify which programs fit your business.

We start with SBA loan programs. The 7(a) program is the SBA's primary lending program — it offers loans up to five million dollars for working capital, equipment, real estate, and business acquisition. The 504 program is designed specifically for major fixed assets like commercial real estate and heavy equipment. And the Microloan program offers loans up to fifty thousand dollars, specifically designed for new and small businesses that may not qualify for traditional bank financing.

We cover what each program requires in terms of time in business, credit history, collateral, and documentation. We also cover SBA-approved lenders — not all banks offer SBA loans, and knowing which lenders are SBA-preferred can significantly speed up your application process.

We then cover SBA certification programs — 8(a), HUBZone, Women-Owned Small Business, and Service-Disabled Veteran-Owned Small Business certifications. These certifications grant your business access to set-aside contracts and sole-source awards that are specifically reserved for qualifying businesses. If your business is eligible for any of these certifications, getting certified is one of the highest-leverage moves you can make.

Finally, we cover SCORE and Small Business Development Centers — free advisory resources offered through the SBA that can provide mentorship, business planning support, and introductions to local funding sources.

The SBA is a resource most business owners underuse. This module changes that.

## MODULE 7 — Federal Contracting
~355 words | Estimated runtime: 2:10–2:20

Federal contracting is one of the largest and most consistent sources of revenue available to small businesses — and most entrepreneurs have no idea how to access it.

The federal government spends over six hundred billion dollars every year purchasing goods and services from private businesses. By law, a significant portion of those contracts — twenty-three percent — are supposed to go to small businesses. That is a mandate, not a goal. Agencies are required to hit that number, which means they are actively looking for qualified small businesses to award contracts to.

Your business can be one of them.

In this module we walk through how federal contracting works, how to find contract opportunities, and how to position your business to win them.

We start with SAM.gov and beta.SAM.gov — the platforms where federal contract opportunities are posted. You will learn how to search for opportunities using your NAICS code, how to read a solicitation, and how to identify which opportunities are set aside for small businesses.

We then cover the difference between open market contracts, small business set-asides, and sole-source awards. Each requires a different approach and a different level of qualification. We walk through the pathway into each category.

We also cover subcontracting. Many small businesses enter federal contracting not by winning prime contracts directly, but by partnering with larger businesses as subcontractors. This is often the fastest path to your first federal contract revenue — and it builds the past performance record that makes you competitive for prime contracts later.

We walk through how to identify prime contractors in your industry who are looking for subcontractors, how to approach those relationships, and what to prepare so that conversation goes well.

Federal contracting is a long game. The businesses that win consistently are the ones that show up consistently — registered, compliant, and positioned correctly. This module gets you into that game.

The contracts are real. The money is real. Let's show you how to go get it.

## MODULE 8 — SBIR/STTR Research and Development Funding
~330 words | Estimated runtime: 2:05–2:15

If your business involves technology, innovation, or research and development, there is a federal funding program specifically built for you — and it is one of the most generous and underutilized programs in the entire government funding ecosystem.

The Small Business Innovation Research program — SBIR — and the Small Business Technology Transfer program — STTR — are federal programs that fund small businesses at the research and development stage. Combined, they represent over three billion dollars in annual awards to small businesses working on technology with commercial and federal application.

These are not loans. They are non-dilutive grants. You do not pay them back. You do not give up equity. You receive funding to do research and development work — and you keep the intellectual property.

In this module we walk through how SBIR and STTR work, which federal agencies participate, and how to determine whether your business qualifies.

SBIR awards come in two phases. Phase I awards are typically one hundred fifty thousand to two hundred fifty thousand dollars, covering a six-month feasibility study or proof of concept. Phase II awards are larger — typically up to one million dollars or more — and fund the full development of the technology. Phase III is commercialization, which the government does not fund directly but which often leads to federal procurement contracts.

STTR is similar to SBIR but requires a partnership with a research institution — a university or a federal laboratory. If you have an academic partner, STTR opens a parallel funding pathway.

We cover how to find open SBIR and STTR solicitations, how to read a solicitation to determine fit, and what a competitive SBIR proposal looks like. We also cover the common mistakes that disqualify otherwise strong proposals.

SBIR and STTR funding is real, competitive, and available to small businesses at every stage of development. If your business involves any form of innovation or technology, this module could be the most valuable one in the entire course.

## MODULE 9 — Stacking Multiple Funding Sources
~340 words | Estimated runtime: 2:05–2:15

The most sophisticated business owners do not rely on one source of funding. They stack. They combine grants with SBA loans, layer federal contracts with business credit, and use early-stage funding to qualify for larger programs. That is what this module is about.

Stacking means strategically combining multiple funding sources so each one strengthens your position for the next. A grant award gives you proof of concept and demonstrated viability — which makes you more attractive to a lender. An SBA loan builds your credit profile — which makes you more competitive for larger grants. A federal contract gives you past performance — which qualifies you for larger contracts and set-aside programs.

None of these are accidents. They are a sequence — and knowing the sequence is what separates businesses that access serious capital from businesses that stay stuck applying for one thing at a time.

In this module we walk through the most effective stacking sequences for different business types. Product businesses, service businesses, technology companies, and real estate operators each have a different optimal path — and we map those paths so you can identify the one that fits where you are right now.

We also cover how to manage multiple applications simultaneously without overwhelming your operation. Grant applications, loan applications, contract bids, and certification processes all have deadlines, documentation requirements, and follow-up steps. We show you how to build a simple funding tracker that keeps your pipeline organized and ensures nothing falls through the cracks.

Finally, we address reinvestment. When early funding comes in, how you deploy it matters. We cover how to use initial grants and contracts to build the infrastructure — credit, accounts, registrations, certifications — that qualifies your business for the next level of funding.

This is the full picture: not just how to get funded once, but how to build a business that keeps accessing capital as it grows.

That is what sustainable funding looks like.
